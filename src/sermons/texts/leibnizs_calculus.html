In the late 17th Century, a mathematical revolution was on the horizon. In 1684, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz published a paper with a the rather long title: Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis, itemque tangentibus, quae nec fractas nec irrationales, quantitates moratur, et singulare pr illi calculi genus. or if you prefer in English A new method for Maxima and Minima as Well as Tangents, Which is Impeded Neither by Fractional Nor by Irrational Quantities, and a Remarkable Type of Calculus for This. Infinitesimals

Leibniz was interested in the tangent line to a point on a curve. One intuitive way to interpret the tangent line to a point on a curve is as the straight line that just touches the curve at that point. This is the approach that Euclid took in the Elements, defining a tanget line as a line such that no other straight line could fall between it and the curve. This geometric definition of the tangent proved to be fairly useful, and Euclid established that the tangent line to a point on a circle is the line perpendicular to the radius line.

The problem was though that this definition did not provide a way for determining the tangent line for a point on an arbitrary curve. Leibniz was aware of this and crucially changed the perspective of the tangent altogether. Given any curve $y = f(x)$, he considered two arbitrary points, $P$ and $Q$ lying on this curve.

[IMAGE HERE]

Leibniz considered the straight line on which $P$ and $Q$ lie on, and wondered, what were to happen to this line should you fix $P$ and move $Q$ toward it, following the path of the curve. It is clear to see that as $Q$ gets closer and closer to P, this line gets closer and closer to the line that just touches the curve at $P$. With this picture in mind, Leibniz postulated that the line on which $P$ and $Q$ lie on was exactly the tangent line at $P$, when $P$ and $Q$ are infinitely close to one another, yet still distinct.

[IMAGE HERE]

It is with this intuitive setup, that Leibniz defined the notion of infinitesimals. Let $P = (x,y)$ and $Q = (x', y')$. If $P$ and $Q$ are infinitely close to one another (bust still distinct!), then $x$ and $x'$ must be inifnitely close to one another (bust still distinct), and similarly for $y$ and $y'$. Leibniz said that in this case, the difference $x - x'$ is infinitely small but not zero. He denoted this infinitesimal difference by $dx$, and similarly $dy$ for the infinitesimal difference between $y$ and $y'$.

Therefore, since the slope of a straight line is the change in $y$ divided by the change in $x$, Leibniz deduced that $\mathrm{d}x$ and $\mathrm{d}y$ are related by the equation $$ \mathrm{d}y = (\text{slope of tangent line at } P) \cdot \mathrm{d}x $$ and by dividing both sides by $\mathrm{d}x$, we obtain the familiar expression $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \text{slope of tangent line at } P $$

Leibniz referred to $\mathrm{d}x$ as a differential which is why we sometimes refer to calculus by differential calculus.

The Leibniz rules

Following this, Leibniz presented his rules of calculus, though without proof. Even in the 17th century proofs were sometimes left as an exercie to the reader! I introduced Leibniz's notion of the differential through considering the slop of the tangent line at a point on a curve, which is the division of two differentials. Therefore it makes sense to give some rules for manipulating these differentials directly and so perhaps a more apt name for these tules is the algebra of differentials. I have stated these rules below almost exxactly as Leibniz stated them in his paper. Indeed , you may find some of them quite familiar. $$ \text{If } a \text{ is a given constant, then } \mathrm{d}a = 0 \text{ and if } y = ax \text{ then } \mathrm{d}y = a\mathrm{d}x. $$ Now, addition and subtraction: $$ \text{if } v = z - y + w + x \text{ then } \mathrm{d}v = \mathrm{d}z - \mathrm{dy} + \mathrm{d}w + \mathrm{d}x $$ Multiplication (product rule): $$ \text{ if } y = vx \text{ then } \mathrm{d}y = v\mathrm{d}x + x\mathrm{d}v $$ and lastly, division (quotient rule): $$ \text{if } z = \frac{v}{y} \text{ then } \mathrm{d}z = \frac{v \mathrm{d}y - y \mathrm{d}v}{yy} $$

Let us do the proof of the product rule using Leibniz's notion of infinitesimals, but I shall leave the rest up to you, as Leibniz intended! Consider two quantities $v, x$ and denote their product $y = vx$. We can interpret this product as an area

We would like to find dy, so let us consider $y + \mathrm{d}y$. We can interpret this as pertubing $y$ by an infinitesimal amount, $\mathrm{d}y$. Since $y = vx$, this pertubation must induce a pertubation in $v$ and $x$ respectively.

IMAGE HERE

Therefore, we can write $$ y + \mathrm{d}y = (v + \mathrm{d}v)(x + \mathrm{d}x) = vx + v\mathrm{d}x + x\mathrm{d}v + \mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}x $$

Stay with me for this next part, or if you're a physicist you probably won't even flinch. Since $\mathrm{d}v$ and $\mathrm{d}x$ are infinitely small, Leibniz reasoned that $\mathrm{d}v \mathrm{d}x$ must be even more infinitely small and therefore we can just sweep it under the rug. Essentially, Leibniz treated the product of infinitsimals as negligible. Therefore, we are left with $$ y + \mathrm{d}y = vx + v\mathrm{d}x + x\mathrm{d}v $$ and since $y = vx$, subtracting y from both sides yields the result, $dy = vdx + xdv$.

Now, you probably felt uneasy in the middle there. After all, how can the product of two infinitely small quantities be even more infinitely small? What does it even mean to be infinitely small if there is something even smaller? Leibniz was also uneasy of his logic, but his methods worked. They provided verifiably correct answers to previously unsolved problems. In fact, the invention of differential calculus was so profound that it opened up an entirely new catalogue of problems for mathematicians of the day to sink their teeth into. One such problem was the brachistrochone problem.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

"We, now mounting to greater heights, obtain the area of a figure by finding the figure of its summatrix or quadratrix". Given a curve $y = f(x)$, we seek to find the area of the region enclosed between the curve and the horizontal $x$-axis. Leibniz considered strips or rectangles of infinitesimal width bounded by the curve.

IMAGE HERE

Leibniz reasoned that the sum of all these rectangles is exactly the area we seek to find. What about the triangles I hear you ask. Akin to his logic for neglecting the product of infinitesimals, Leibniz reasoned that the area of the triangles are negligeble compared to the area of the rectangles. They surely had smaller height compared to the rectangles and smaller width, therefore they must be infinitely smaller than the already infinitely small rectangles!

Mathematically, the area of one of these recangles is simply $y \cdot \mathrm{d}x$. And since the area is the sum of all such rectangles we deduce $$ \text{Area} = \sum y\mathrm{d}x $$ where omn is short for omnia in latin which translates to "all" or "everything". Unfortunately this formula isn't very useful to us. It doesn't actually give us a way of computing the area, we'd have to take a sum of infinitely many things that are infinitely small.

We now mount to the greater heights that Leibniz foreshadowed. Leibniz considered the problem from an entirely different perspective. He started by supposing the existence of a curve $z = g(x)$ such that its derivative function is exactly $y = f(x)$ $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}x} = y $$ Remember, to Leibniz $\frac{dz}{dx}$ was actually a fraction, and so he re-arranged the equation for $$ \mathrm{d}z = y\mathrm{dx} $$ Geometrically, $y\mathrm{d}x$ is the area of one of the rectangles bounded by the curve $y$ of infinitely small width $dx$. Therefore, if we sum both sides, we obtain the area swept by the curve $y$. $$ Area = \sum y\mathrm{d}x = \sum dz = z $$ Here Leibniz imparts the final component of his calculus notation. He introduces the integral. $$ Area = \int y \mathrm{d}x = z $$ Leibniz had "derived" the fundamental theorem of calculus. I cannot express the profoundness of his discovery. $$ \frac{dz}{dx} = y \quad z = \int y\mathrm{d}x $$

The brachistrochone problem

In 1696, Johann Bernoulli famously posed the following problem to the mathematicians of Europe. I, Johann Bernoulli, address the most brilliant mathematicians in the world. Nothing is more attractive to intelligent people than an honest, challenging problem, whose possible solution will bestow fame and remain as a lasting monument. Following the example set by Pascal, Fermat, etc., I hope to gain the gratitude of the whole scientific community by placing before the finest mathematicians of our time a problem which will test their methods and the strength of their intellect. If someone communicates to me the solution of the proposed problem, I shall publicly declare him worthy of praise.

The problem he posed was the following. Given two points $A$ and $B$ in a vertical plane, what is the curve traced out by a point acted on only by gravity, which starts at $A$ and reaches $B$ in the shortest time?

Bernoulli initially set a 6 month deadline for his contemporaries to send in their solutions to the brachistrochone problem. Through persuasion by Leibniz, Bernoulli extended this deadline to allow foreign mathematicians to have a chance at solving the problem. In the end five solutions were obtained alongside Johann Bernoulli's, Leibniz, Guillaume de L'Hopital, Jacob Bernoulli, and Sir Isaac Newton.

At this point, Newton had abandoned mathematical reserach and taken up a position in the government as Warden of the Royal Mint. According to Newton's biographer, one day upon returning home from the Royal Mint, he recieved Bernoulli's letter and proceeded to work through the evening and early morning until he solved the brachistrochone problem. Newton sent his solution anonymously to Bernoulli who immediately recognised the work as Newton's. It is said that Bernoulli remarked Tanquam ex ungue leonem. I recognised the lion by his claws.
