In the late 17th Century, a mathematical revolution was on the horizon. In 1684, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz published a paper with a the rather long title: Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis, itemque tangentibus, quae nec fractas nec irrationales, quantitates moratur, et singulare pr illi calculi genus. or if you prefer in English A new method for Maxima and Minima as Well as Tangents, Which is Impeded Neither by Fractional Nor by Irrational Quantities, and a Remarkable Type of Calculus for This. Leibniz was interested in the tangent line to a point on a curve. One intuitive way to interpret the tangent line to a point on a curve is as the straight line that just touches the curve at that point. This is the approach that Euclid took in the Elements, defining a tanget line as a line such that no other straight line could fall between it and the curve. This geometric definition of the tangent proved to be fairly useful, and Euclid established that the tangent line to a point on a circle is the line perpendicular to the radius line. The problem was though that this definition did not provide a way for determining the tangent line for a point on an arbitrary curve. Leibniz was aware of this and crucially changed the perspective of the tangent altogether. Given any curve y = f(x), he considered two arbitrary points, P and Q lying on this curve.
